About this project

When governments weigh an environmental policy — cleaner electricity, protecting forests from an invasive pest — they often rely on surveys that ask people directly: would you support this policy if it cost your household $X per year? This method, called contingent valuation (CV), underpins many official benefit–cost analyses. But fielding such surveys is slow and expensive, and researchers have begun asking whether large language models could stand in for human respondents.

This project puts that idea to a direct test. We gave ten LLMs personas built from real survey participants — their age, income, education, and (in one condition) their stated beliefs — and had them answer the same questionnaires from two published CV studies: a national survey on a U.S. clean energy standard (Aldy et al., 2012) and a North Carolina survey on protecting hemlock forests from an invasive pest (Giguere et al., 2020). Because both original studies report household willingness to pay (WTP), the "synthetic" answers can be compared directly against real human benchmarks.

The short answer: not yet — and the ways the models miss are systematic, not random. The interactive charts below let you compare every model against the human benchmarks yourself.

Key finding

Synthetic contingent valuation does not recover human-survey benchmarks under naive deployment. Across both studies and almost every model, synthetic approval-bid curves are systematically flatter than empirical curves — a pattern with direct mechanical consequences for willingness-to-pay estimation through the standard logit framework. Across-LLM heterogeneity is large enough that model selection is a first-order design decision; ensemble averaging does not rescue weak models.

Methods

What is synthetic CV?

Contingent valuation asks respondents whether they would support a policy at a given cost. In synthetic CV, large language models stand in for human respondents: given a persona describing a real survey participant's demographics and beliefs, the model is prompted to vote as that person would. The question we ask is whether the response patterns LLMs produce approximate the patterns human respondents produce closely enough to support welfare analysis.

What is Alethic-ISM?

All simulations were executed on Alethic-ISM, an open-source AI research workbench that represents computational experiments as directed graphs of immutable, versioned states. Each node applies an instruction to an input state — in this case, prompting an LLM with a synthetic persona — producing a new state linked to its full provenance chain. The platform supports parallel execution across multiple models and maintains a permanent audit trail of every API call. Available at github.com/alethicresearch/alethic-ism.

How to read the plots

The forest plots show mean WTP estimates (points) with 95% confidence intervals (lines) for each LLM, in two conditions: demographics-only personas (red diamonds) and demographics-plus-beliefs personas (blue squares). Vertical dashed lines mark the human-survey benchmark and its confidence interval. The approval curve plots show share of synthetic respondents supporting the policy at each bid level; the red line is empirical, blue dashed lines are synthetic. The slope compression chart shows the ratio of each model's bid coefficient to the empirical bid coefficient — values below 1 indicate synthetic respondents are less price-sensitive than human respondents.
